Redrawing the World: How the 2026 UN Resolution to Abandon the Mercator Projection Turns a Map Into a Lesson on Power, Truth, and English Vocabulary

Based on the visual presentation Redrawing the World — The 2026 UN Resolution to Abandon the Mercator Projection.

📎 Original article: this material accompanies the ABC News report “Africa bigger, US smaller: United Nations adopts new world map”, published on 5 September 2026.

Introduction: A World Seen Through a Mathematical Trick

For almost five hundred years, the world has been looking at itself through a mathematical trick. On the wall maps and in the phone apps most of us grew up with, Greenland sprawls like a continent and Africa looks like a slightly larger island — even though the real Africa is fourteen times larger than Greenland. The presentation Redrawing the World — The 2026 UN Resolution to Abandon the Mercator Projection takes this quiet distortion and turns it into a dramatic story of science, history, and diplomacy.

The material, a thirteen-frame visual presentation, follows a real diplomatic event covered by ABC News: in September 2026, the UN General Assembly voted to replace the traditional Mercator projection with the Equal Earth map. For English learners it is a rare three-in-one resource: a geography lesson about how images shape perception, a history lesson about a tool invented for sixteenth-century sailors, and a vocabulary lesson full of the diplomatic English that appears in the news every single week. Pay attention to how much language is packed into a topic that, at first glance, seems to be only about maps.

🗺️ Section 1 — The Story Behind the Resolution: Content and Context

A New Cartographic Standard

The heart of the story is announced in a single line: “In September 2026, the UN General Assembly passed a landmark resolution to replace the traditional Mercator projection with the Equal Earth map, aiming for a more accurate representation of continental landmasses.” The frame labels the two eras with museum-like precision: “The Outgoing Standard: Mercator Projection (Est. 1569)” and “The Incoming Standard: Equal Earth Projection (Est. 2018).” Notice the verbs that carry the main sentence — passed, replace, aiming — because together they form the classic grammar of formal announcements: an institution, an act, a change, and a goal.

The Vote: A Global Consensus with a Singular Dissent

The vote is presented under a headline worth memorizing as a ready-made phrase: “A Global Consensus with a Singular Dissent.” The numbers appear as a field of colored dots: “164 For (Spearheaded by African nations),” “6 Abstentions,” and “1 Against (United States).” One small dot, ringed in red, carries the entire opposition. The design makes the politics instantly readable: a near-unanimous world, a handful of neutrals, and a single holdout.

💡 Practical advice: retell any vote with the three basic verbs — to support, to oppose, to abstain — and the three matching nouns — support, opposition, abstention. The diagram teaches the complete voting vocabulary set in one glance, and it transfers to student councils, parliaments, and boardrooms alike.

The 1569 Original: A Tool Worth Respecting

Before judging the old map, the presentation asks the reader to respect it: “Designed by Flemish cartographer Gerardus Mercator, the projection prioritized accurate depictions of shapes and angles. This mathematical design allowed sailors to plot straight-line courses (rhumb lines) across oceans, a revolutionary tool for 16th-century global trade.” This is a masterclass in balanced writing: the frame acknowledges the tool’s brilliance before exposing its cost, and that structure — credit first, critique second — is a pattern every learner can copy in essays and debates.

⚠️ Important nuance: the resolution does not declare the Mercator projection wrong; it reassigns it. A map designed to keep navigation angles straight remains exactly that — a navigation tool — while a map of the world for classrooms, textbooks, and newsrooms needs a different promise. Remember that most disagreements in English are softened this way: the frame attacks the application, not the invention.

The Distortion Problem, Measured Honestly

The mechanism is explained with textbook clarity: “To keep maritime navigation angles straight, the projection artificially inflates northern regions and compresses equatorial zones, severely distorting relative landmass sizes.” Flattening a globe onto a rectangle always forces a choice, and Mercator chose angles over area. The key point is that the distortion is not a mistake — it is the price of the projection’s one great virtue.

The consequence is summarized in the most memorable fact of the whole deck: “Under the Mercator projection, Greenland and Africa appear to be roughly the same size. In reality, Africa is 14 times larger.” The visual hammers the point home by packing fourteen tiny Greenland silhouettes into the outline of Africa. As a real-life exercise, the image invites a simple classroom experiment: print both shapes, cut out Greenland, and count how many times it fits inside its African shadow — fourteen, every time, no matter how many people repeat the test.

The 2018 Correction: Equal Earth

The answer arrived in 2018, when a team of cartographers proposed a fairer geometry. The frame quotes the projection’s creator directly: “Equal Earth preserves the relative surface areas of continents and, as much as possible, shows their shapes as they appear on a globe.” Below the quote, the deck adds its own interpretation: “Launched by a group of cartographers in 2018, this projection restores visual equity to the Southern Hemisphere.” The verb restores deserves special attention: it implies that fairness was the planet’s default state and that the flat map had temporarily taken it away.

Two Standards, Side by Side

A comparison frame lines the two projections up on four criteria, and the contrast reads almost like a pair of biographies:

  • Era & Origin: Mercator — 1569 (Gerardus Mercator); Equal Earth — 2018 (Bojan Savric & team).
  • Primary Purpose: Mercator — Maritime Navigation (plotting straight courses); Equal Earth — Accurate Representation (true comparative scale).
  • Geometric Focus: Mercator — Preserves Shapes & Angles; Equal Earth — Preserves Relative Surface Area.
  • Spatial Distortion: Mercator — Inflates Poles, Compresses Equator; Equal Earth — Proportional Continental Landmasses.

📌 The key point is that neither projection is universally superior; each one is faithful to a different property of the planet. Choosing a map means choosing which truth to prioritize — a sentence that works just as well for language learning as for cartography.

The Shifting Silhouettes

The frame titled “The Shifting Silhouettes” shows what actually changes when the standard changes. The United States and Europe are marked SHRINKING — “(Poleward Inflation Reduced)” and “(Reduced Distortion)” — Greenland is marked DRAMATIC SHRINKING — “(Extreme Polar Inflation Corrected)” — while Africa receives PROPORTIONAL EXPANSION — “(True Area Revealed)” — and Australia receives EXPANSION — “(Visual Equity Restored).” Notice that the deck chooses different verbs for different hemispheres: the Global North shrinks, while Africa and Australia expand — a small linguistic asymmetry that carries the entire argument.

The Diplomatic Friction of Cartography

The presentation then widens the lens from geometry to geopolitics. On one side of an old-fashioned balance scale sit the flags of Togo and France, weighted with the label Cognitive Justice & Truth and the words of Togo’s Foreign Minister: “[Maps] guide education, nourish the imagination… they are therefore never neutral.” On the other side sits the flag of the United States with the label Institutional Credibility & Genuine Problems and the objection of US Representative Yaryna Ferencevych: “…debating map projects from the 16th century and their role in promoting reparations and cognitive justice.” The frame captures a genuine philosophical divide: one camp sees the map as a moral document, while the other sees the debate itself as a distraction from practical problems.

France’s position adds a layer of strategy: “France co-sponsored the resolution to abandon Mercator as part of a broader initiative to improve prickly relations with former African colonies.” The same frame reports the argument of Foreign Minister Jean-Noel Barrot: “under Mercator, the US, Russia, and China took on a ‘disproportionate visual prominence’ compared to Africa, Latin America, or South-East Asia. Correcting this was a necessary diplomatic signal.” Diplomacy, the deck suggests, can be conducted with an atlas.

👩‍🏫 A lesson-design observation: the material follows a classic teaching arc — a striking hook, a historical background, a mathematical problem, a modern solution, a political debate, and a memorable closing quote. That arc is a template worth borrowing for any presentation, essay, or retelling practice.

📖 Section 2 — Vocabulary and Language Takeaways

The deck doubles as a compact dictionary of formal and journalistic English. The phrases below come straight from the frames, and each one belongs to one of three families: the language of official decisions, the language of navigation, and the language of fairness.

1. a landmark resolution 📌

Literal meaning: a landmark is a tall, visible object — a tower, a mountain — that helps travelers fix their position; a resolution is a formal written decision adopted by an official body.

Figurative meaning: a decision so significant that it will serve as a reference point in history.

From the material: “In September 2026, the UN General Assembly passed a landmark resolution to replace the traditional Mercator projection with the Equal Earth map.” The adjective tells the reader, before any details arrive, that this decision will be remembered.

How the material teaches it: the headline above the quote — “The United Nations Adopts a New Cartographic Standard” — pairs the noun resolution with two verbs worth memorizing: to pass and to adopt.

2. to spearhead

Literal meaning: the metal point at the very front of a spear — the first part to reach the enemy.

Figurative meaning: to lead a campaign or initiative; to be the driving force at its front.

From the material: “164 For (Spearheaded by African nations).” The label credits African countries with leading the initiative rather than merely supporting it.

How the material teaches it: the phrase appears as a compact passive — spearheaded by + the agent — a pattern that lets formal writing name the driving force in two words.

3. rhumb lines

Literal meaning: a nautical term for a line that crosses every meridian at the same angle; on a Mercator map such a line is perfectly straight, which made it priceless for sailors.

Figurative meaning: a steady, unambiguous course through complicated terrain.

From the material: “This mathematical design allowed sailors to plot straight-line courses (rhumb lines) across oceans, a revolutionary tool for 16th-century global trade.”

How the material teaches it: the technical term stands in parentheses right beside its plain-language definition, straight-line courses — an elegant, instantly copyable technique for explaining any specialist word.

4. an abstention / to abstain

Literal meaning: from the Latin abstinere, “to hold back”; in voting, a conscious decision to choose neither yes nor no.

Figurative meaning: a deliberate neutrality; a refusal to be counted on either side.

From the material: “6 Abstentions” appears beside “1 Against (United States),” and the gray dots on the diagram sit visibly apart from the blue and green ones.

How the material teaches it: by showing all three voting positions in one picture — For, Against, Abstentions — the deck teaches the complete voting vocabulary set without a single line of definitions.

5. disproportionate visual prominence

Literal meaning: disproportionate means out of proportion — too large or too small relative to something else; prominence means the quality of standing out, of being easily noticed.

Figurative meaning: overexposure — being seen as bigger, louder, or more important than the facts justify.

From the material: Foreign Minister Jean-Noel Barrot noted that under Mercator, the US, Russia, and China took on a “disproportionate visual prominence” compared to Africa, Latin America, or South-East Asia.

How the material teaches it: the frame adds its own interpretation — “Correcting this was a necessary diplomatic signal” — showing how diplomats convert a visual complaint into a formal message.

6. prickly relations

Literal meaning: prickly describes a surface covered in fine sharp spines, like a cactus or a hedgehog.

Figurative meaning: easily irritated, tense, and quick to take offense — the perfect adjective for delicate diplomacy.

From the material: “France co-sponsored the resolution to abandon Mercator as part of a broader initiative to improve prickly relations with former African colonies.”

How the material teaches it: the metaphor sits inside a chain of diplomatic vocabulary — co-sponsored, initiative, former colonies — so learners absorb the register of international relations in a single sentence.

7. cognitive justice

Literal meaning: cognitive relates to the mind — knowing, perceiving, imagining; justice means fairness.

Figurative meaning: fairness in what entire populations are taught to see, know, and imagine about the world.

From the material: “[Maps] guide education, nourish the imagination… they are therefore never neutral.” — Robert Dussey, Foreign Minister of Togo.

How the material teaches it: the phrase is displayed on one pan of a balance scale under the heading Cognitive Justice & Truth, so the abstract concept is anchored to a concrete visual of weighing.

8. visual equity

Literal meaning: equity is fairness — not sameness, but each part receiving its due.

Figurative meaning: fair proportions in images; giving the Southern Hemisphere its true visual weight.

From the material: “Launched by a group of cartographers in 2018, this projection restores visual equity to the Southern Hemisphere.” Later, Australia’s new silhouette is labeled “(Visual Equity Restored).”

How the material teaches it: the verb restore does the teaching — it implies that fairness was the planet’s original condition and that the flat map had quietly taken it away.

Notice how much of the deck is written in the passive voice — was passed, was designed, was launched, is preserved, is revealed — because formal English often hides the actor in order to emphasize the act. Notice also the collocations worth memorizing as whole chunks: pass a resolution, adopt a standard, co-sponsor a resolution, plot a course, restore equity. Learning them as ready-made pairs is faster and more accurate than translating word by word, and each of them connects directly back to the theme of the material: decisions, directions, and fairness.

🧭 Section 3 — Deeper Meaning: Metaphors and Symbols

1. The Peeling Map: Two Worlds in One Frame

The cover splits the screen down the middle: on the left, an aged parchment chart complete with compass roses and hand-drawn sea monsters; on the right, a glowing digital Earth wrapped in data. The second frame extends the image by showing the old page peeling away from the corner to reveal the new map beneath, labeled “The Outgoing Standard: Mercator Projection (Est. 1569)” and “The Incoming Standard: Equal Earth Projection (Est. 2018).” The symbol is precise: progress does not tear the old world up; it peels it back gently, layer by layer, and lets the newer truth show through. For learners, the image doubles as advice — replacing an old mental model does not require destroying it, only seeing it as one layer among others.

2. The Balance Scale: Cartography as a Negotiation

The debate frame borrows the oldest symbol of justice: a set of scales weighing “Cognitive Justice & Truth” against “Institutional Credibility & Genuine Problems,” with national flags as the weights. The visual argument is elegant, because a scale does not declare a winner; it holds two claims in tension and forces the viewer to decide what carries more weight. The frame’s quotes sharpen that tension: “[Maps] guide education, nourish the imagination… they are therefore never neutral.” set against the US objection about “debating map projects from the 16th century and their role in promoting reparations and cognitive justice.” English offers a whole family of scale idioms — to weigh the options, to tip the scales, to strike a balance — and this frame gives every one of them a concrete picture.

3. The Three Circles: The Math, The Mind, The Power

A synthesis frame organizes the whole story into three overlapping circles: The Math — “Solving the 3D-to-2D geometric distortion”; The Mind — “Addressing ‘Cognitive Justice’ by removing the systemic inflation of the Global North”; The Power — “Shifting visual prominence away from a Eurocentric worldview to reflect the true physical weight of the Global South.” The diagram teaches a transferable analytical habit: any object — a map, a word, a media feed — can be examined as a technical artifact, a psychological influence, and an instrument of power. The frame seals the argument with its most quotable line: “The 2026 UN resolution proves that global maps are not merely geographic tools, but active political instruments that shape international relations.”

4. An Act of Truth

The final frame carries a single sentence over a full-width Equal Earth globe: “Changing maps obviously doesn’t change the world. But correcting a distorted representation is already an act of truth.” — Jean-Noel Barrot, 2026. The metaphor elevates a technical correction into an ethical gesture. Distortion here stops being a cartographic term and becomes a moral one, which mirrors everyday English: people speak of a distorted picture, a true-to-life account, a fair representation when they discuss honesty in words as well as in images. The map, in the end, is a metaphor for every representation we trust.

🌍 Conclusion: The Map You Choose Is the World You See

The material ends where good lessons always end — with a sentence worth keeping: “Changing maps obviously doesn’t change the world. But correcting a distorted representation is already an act of truth.” For language learners the takeaway reaches far beyond geography: the frames, the words, and the map you choose all frame reality, and choosing them consciously is itself a skill. A learner who can explain the reason Greenland looks enormous on a classroom wall while knowing that it is fourteen times smaller than Africa has already redrawn an internal map — and the same principle applies to vocabulary, where the right phrase can correct a distorted impression faster than any grammar rule.

📌 Open the full presentation, retell the story aloud using at least five phrases from the vocabulary list above, and place a Mercator map next to an Equal Earth map to compare them with your own eyes. Then spend one week noticing the language of decisions in the news — landmark, spearheaded, abstention, disproportionate, restore — and observe how quickly these once-exotic words start to feel like old friends. Truth, like vocabulary, is restored one correction at a time.

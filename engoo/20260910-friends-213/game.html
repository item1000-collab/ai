📚 The Vocabulary Field Guide

Open the field guide — every phrase from the night, with the material’s own definitions

  • 📌 Sitcom (noun)

    Literal meaning: short for “situation comedy” — a television series with a fixed cast and a repeating setting.

    In this story: the entire journey lives inside one; the cover calls it “the biggest sitcom episode of the 1990s.”

    Example from the material: “Master essential English phrases through the biggest sitcom episode of the 1990s” — the word frames the whole lesson.

    How the material explains it: “Sitcom (n): A ‘situation comedy’ television series.” The definition unpacks the blend into its two source words.

  • 📌 Broadcast (noun / verb)

    Literal meaning: a television or radio program, or the act of sending it out to an audience.

    In this story: it names the act of beaming one episode to the entire country on a single January night.

    Example from the material: “Aired on January 28, 1996, immediately following Super Bowl XXX” — the airing itself is the broadcast in action.

    How the material explains it: “Broadcast (n/v): A television or radio program.” The slash signals that the same word works as both a noun and a verb.

  • 📌 Record-breaking (adjective)

    Literal meaning: surpassing all previous achievements.

    In this story: the episode’s 52.9 million viewers set the series’ all-time mark.

    Example from the material: “52.9 Million: Total viewers (most-watched in series history)” — the statistic is the broken record made visible.

    How the material explains it: “Record-breaking (adj): Surpassing all previous achievements.” A compact definition that turns a hyphenated compound into a verb phrase.

  • 📌 Guest star (noun)

    Literal meaning: a famous actor appearing temporarily in a show.

    In this story: the Hollywood talent imported to make the special episode shine.

    Example from the material: “designed to attract massive advertising revenue by bringing in top Hollywood talent” — those imported actors are the guest stars.

    How the material explains it: “Guest star (n): A famous actor appearing temporarily in a show.” The word “temporarily” separates a guest from the regular cast.

  • 📌 Reunion (noun)

    Literal meaning: coming together again after being separated.

    In this story: what Ross spends the whole plot chasing with Marcel.

    Example from the material: “Ross travels to the San Diego Zoo to visit his former pet monkey, Marcel” — the trip is a search for a reunion.

    How the material explains it: “Reunion (n): Coming together again after being separated.” The prefix “re-” carries the idea of doing something again.

  • 📌 Administrator (noun)

    Literal meaning: a person who manages an organization.

    In this story: the zoo official who delivers the false news about Marcel.

    Example from the material: “An administrator claims Marcel died” — the lie comes from the manager of the institution itself.

    How the material explains it: “Administrator (n): A person who manages an organization.” A neutral office word placed inside an emotional scene.

  • 📌 Kidnapped (verb)

    Literal meaning: taken away illegally by force.

    In this story: Marcel’s true fate, revealed by the janitor.

    Example from the material: “Marcel was kidnapped and forced into show business” — one word carries the whole plot twist.

    How the material explains it: “Kidnapped (v): Taken away illegally by force.” The definition stresses both the illegality and the force.

  • 📌 Show business (noun)

    Literal meaning: the entertainment industry — movies, theater, TV.

    In this story: the glittering machine that turned a stolen pet into a movie star.

    Example from the material: “Marcel is starring in a major Hollywood movie” — show business in its shiniest form.

    How the material explains it: “Show business (n): The entertainment industry (movies, theater, TV).” The parentheses list the three stages the phrase covers.

  • 📌 Prank (noun)

    Literal meaning: a trick played on someone as a joke.

    In this story: Chandler’s cruel 4th-grade trick on Susie — the seed of the whole plot.

    Example from the material: “In 4th grade, Chandler pulled a cruel prank on her” — the word “cruel” darkens the usual playfulness.

    How the material explains it: “Prank (n): A trick played on someone as a joke.” The definition shows why the revenge that follows feels so pointed.

  • 📌 Make out (verb, informal)

    Literal meaning: to kiss passionately.

    In this story: Chandler and Susie’s moment in the restaurant bathroom — the doorway to the trap.

    Example from the material: “they make out in a restaurant bathroom” — the informal phrasal verb signals casual, spoken English.

    How the material explains it: “Make out (v, informal): To kiss passionately.” The label “informal” warns learners to keep it out of formal writing.

  • 📌 Revenge (noun)

    Literal meaning: action taken to hurt someone because they hurt you.

    In this story: Susie’s stolen clothes — harm returned for a childhood harm.

    Example from the material: “Susie steals his clothes as an act of revenge, leaving him in terrible embarrassment” — the sentence chains revenge to its result.

    How the material explains it: “Revenge (n): Action taken to hurt someone because they hurt you.” The definition mirrors the “hurt for a hurt” structure of the plot.

  • 📌 Embarrassment (noun)

    Literal meaning: a feeling of shame, self-consciousness, or awkwardness.

    In this story: what Chandler is left holding when the bathroom door opens.

    Example from the material: “leaving him in terrible embarrassment” — the feeling becomes the scene’s punchline.

    How the material explains it: “Embarrassment (n): A feeling of shame, self-consciousness, or awkwardness.” Three near-synonyms shade the single feeling.

  • 📌 Smitten (adjective)

    Literal meaning: strongly attracted to someone.

    In this story: Monica’s condition in front of the action star — struck, and speechless.

    Example from the material: “Monica is smitten but too shy to speak to him” — the attraction is instant and paralyzing.

    How the material explains it: “Smitten (adj): Strongly attracted to someone.” The word originally meant “struck,” and the definition keeps that sudden force.

  • 📌 Tension (noun)

    Literal meaning: mental or emotional strain; bad feelings between people.

    In this story: the charge in the air between two best friends.

    Example from the material: “This creates fierce tension and a bitter rivalry” — the sentence pairs the feeling with its consequence.

    How the material explains it: “Tension (n): Mental or emotional strain; bad feelings between people.” The semicolon offers both the inner and the outer reading.

  • 📌 Rivalry (noun)

    Literal meaning: competition for the same objective or superiority.

    In this story: Monica versus Rachel, with one famous actor as the prize.

    Example from the material: “a bitter rivalry” stretched across one borrowed sweater.

    How the material explains it: “Rivalry (n): Competition for the same objective or superiority.” The definition names both the shared goal and the fight for rank.

  • 📌 Threesome (noun)

    Literal meaning: any group of three; in the material, a romantic encounter involving three people.

    In this story: the actor’s suggestion that ends the fight by ending everything.

    Example from the material: “They fight until he suggests a threesome, at which point both women dump him” — the word marks the exact turning point.

    How the material explains it: “Threesome (n): A romantic encounter involving three people.” The definition keeps the meaning anchored to this episode’s context.

  • 📌 Audition (noun)

    Literal meaning: an interview for a role as an actor or musician.

    In this story: Joey’s desperate, driving goal for the entire episode.

    Example from the material: “Joey tries desperately to get an audition for the movie” — the adverb shows how much rides on it.

    How the material explains it: “Audition (n): An interview for a role as an actor or musician.” The definition frames it as a job interview in costume.

  • 📌 Overacting (verb)

    Literal meaning: performing a role in an exaggerated, unnatural way.

    In this story: the exact reason Joey loses his one and only line.

    Example from the material: “loses his only line due to severe overacting” — the adjective “severe” measures the damage.

    How the material explains it: “Overacting (v): Performing a role in an exaggerated, unnatural way.” The prefix “over-” signals acting past its natural limit.

  • 📌 Stalker (noun)

    Literal meaning: a person who obsessively harasses or follows someone.

    In this story: Joey’s date, who cannot separate the actor from the fictional doctor.

    Example from the material: “a woman who turns out to be a stalker—she believes Joey is actually the fictional doctor he plays on a soap opera” — the dash delivers the reveal.

    How the material explains it: “Stalker (n): A person who obsessively harasses or follows someone.” The adverb “obsessively” is the heart of the definition.

  • 📌 Keep your panties on (idiom / slang)

    Literal meaning: the words name underwear — and Chandler, trapped in the bathroom, is literally wearing them.

    Figurative meaning: a slang way to say “calm down” or “be patient.”

    Example from the material: “He asks for milk, and a friend replies, ‘Keep your panties on.’” — the corkboard circles it in red and shouts GREAT DOUBLE MEANING!

    How the material explains it: “A slang expression meaning ‘calm down’ or ‘be patient.’ (Here, it is used as a double-meaning joke because Chandler is literally wearing them).” The board explains both the idiom and the joke in one breath.

  • 📌 Hamming it up (phrasal verb / idiom)

    Literal meaning: the image comes from “ham” — old show-business slang for a performer who devours the spotlight.

    Figurative meaning: to overact; to perform in an exaggerated, theatrical way to get attention.

    Example from the material: “Joey is playing a dying plague victim, but won’t stop hamming up the scene by moaning and moving too much” — the behavior is the definition in motion.

    How the material explains it: “Meaning: To overact; to perform in an exaggerated, theatrical way to get attention.” The board underlines “overact” and ties the idiom straight back to the backlot plot.
